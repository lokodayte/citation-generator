Site blocked or details missing? Use the Cite this page button

Some sites (the New York Times, for example) refuse requests from this app. This button reads the article from your own browser instead, so it works on anything you can open, and it also sees bylines that only appear once a page has loaded.

  1. Show your bookmarks bar (Chrome: ⌘⇧B), then drag this button onto it: Cite this page
  2. Open the article you want to cite and click the bookmark. This site opens with the citation filled in.

Only the page’s details and the start of its text are sent here, to read the citation from. Nothing is saved.

      What this checks

      Error means the text breaks a rule of standard written English, and there is usually a one-click fix. Check means it is probably worth changing, but it depends on what you mean or on your assignment, so you decide. Text inside quotation marks is left alone (you must quote sources exactly), except for possible misspellings and repeated words. A Works Cited or References list at the end isn’t checked.

      Every rule comes from a published authority: Merriam-Webster (the dictionary MLA and APA use) and the Britannica Dictionary for word usage and informal labels; the APA Style and MLA Style Center websites; and college writing guides (Purdue OWL, UNC Writing Center, Brandeis Writing Program, The Bedford Handbook). Words the dictionary accepts, such as “very unique,” “try and,” and “a lot of,” are not flagged.

        Spelling uses an American English dictionary (Hunspell and SCOWL word lists). Names and technical terms may show as possible misspellings: choose Add to dictionary and they won’t be flagged again in this browser.

        No automatic checker catches every mistake, especially ones that depend on meaning. Deep grammar check uses LanguageTool to find more (for example “He go to school”), and its suggestions are always marked as checks for you to decide.

        Proofread it yourself, too
        • Read it aloud, or use the Read aloud button and follow along. You will hear missing words, repeated words, and sentences that run on.
        • Take a break first. After some time away, you read what is on the page, not what you meant to write.
        • Look for one kind of error at a time, starting with the ones you know you make.
        • Read backward, sentence by sentence, to look at each one on its own.
        • Check names, numbers, dates, and quotations against your sources; no checker can.
        • Match every in-text citation to an entry in your Works Cited or References list, and the other way round.
